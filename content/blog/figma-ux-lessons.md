I'm not a designer. My job title has never said "UX" anywhere in it. But Figma has quietly become one of the most useful tools in my day-to-day — not because I'm great at making things look good, but because a clickable prototype makes an idea easy to explain in a way that words on a ticket never do.

*(Quick heads up: if you're here for Figma shortcuts or clever component workarounds, you're in the wrong place — this isn't that kind of post.)*

## Why prototypes beat descriptions

The clearest example I have: we had a big business requirements document — a BOD, as we called it — pages of text plus a tangled flowchart trying to capture an entire feature. It was the kind of document everyone had technically read, and nobody could fully explain back to you. I built a simple prototype in Figma instead, and that's what actually moved the discussion forward. People stopped debating what the flowchart meant and started reacting to something concrete. That prototype is what got the conversation unstuck — and I ended up taking it from that first mockup all the way to building out the real, scaled application.

It wasn't the first time I'd seen this play out. Back when I was TAing web development at UIUC, sketching quick demos did the same thing for students trying to picture a solution before they'd written any code. The pattern was always the same: a rough clickable mockup got people aligned in five minutes that a written description couldn't get them aligned on in five emails. You stop arguing about what someone meant and start reacting to something that's actually in front of you.

*(If you want to see the same principle from the build side — a wireframe catching a confusing feature before it ever shipped — I wrote about that in my [Tutorly case study](/projects/tutorly).)*

## The book that put a name on it

I recently read *Beyond UX Design: Master Your Craft Beyond Pixels and Prototypes* by Jeremy Miller and Tom Greever, and it reframed something I'd been doing instinctively without really naming it. Their whole argument is that the "craft" part of UX — the pixels, the prototypes, the visual polish — isn't actually where most of the value is. UX isn't just about crafting a beautiful interface. It doesn't matter how thoughtfully laid out the screen is, or how attractive the typography or color scheme is — none of that is actually the point. The point is creating a meaningful outcome for the people using it, and a huge part of getting there is how you work with the people around you, not just the pixels themselves. The real work is in the relationships around it: how you manage stakeholders, how you communicate across teams, how you build the kind of trust that gets your work actually shipped instead of debated forever.

That landed for me because it's exactly what my own Figma habit turned out to be about. I was never trying to be a great visual designer. I was using the prototype as a communication tool — a way to get a product manager, a backend engineer, or a room of students on the same page faster than I could with words alone.

## The takeaway

You don't need to be a "UX person" to get real value out of prototyping tools. If you're an engineer who's tired of Slack threads going in circles over what a feature should actually do, sketch it. If your team is stuck rereading the same requirements doc without actually agreeing on what it means, that's exactly the moment to open Figma instead of writing another paragraph. It doesn't need to be pretty. It just needs to be something people can point at and react to — that's the whole trick.
