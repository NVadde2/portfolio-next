---
title: "My Journey with Reading"
date: "2026-08-19"
description: "From Famous Five in 5th grade to journaling quotes that heal — a lifelong reading habit."
---

Growing up, I read *Snow White*, *Cinderella*, and *Jack and the Beanstalk* more times than I can count — the kind of stories you go back to just because you already know you love them. Then in 5th grade, the library issued me my first book — *Famous Five* by Enid Blyton — and I fell in love instantly. It was pure fiction escapism, living vicariously through their adventures. I finished the entire series faster than the library could keep up with, and started pestering my mom to just buy me the books instead of waiting to check them out again and again.

From there I branched into everything — I read a lot of Shakespeare in translation and found his work genuinely interesting, especially *The Merchant of Venice*, which we studied in 10th grade and went through scene by scene in painstaking detail. There's a reason his plays are still being read and performed centuries later — few writers have made human nature feel that legible. Then came biographies, thrillers by Sidney Sheldon and Agatha Christie, the kind of books you pick up in the morning and don't put down until the last page. My bachelor's degree put reading on pause for a while, but I found my way back not long after. Amish Tripathi's trilogies were what pulled me back in.

These days, deciding what to read next is its own small ritual. I'm a mood reader more than a planner — I don't work through a list so much as pick whatever fits how I'm feeling that week. Sometimes that means wandering a library, walking the shelves until I hit the end of a section. Sometimes it's reading reviews and summaries online, or getting curious about what other book lovers in my book club are picking up. And when nothing new is grabbing me, I'll just go back and re-read an old favorite instead of forcing a new one. I track it all on Goodreads, partly to remember what I've read and partly because seeing what friends are rating is its own way of finding the next book.

What's changed since I was a kid with a library card is what I do with a book after I finish it. I screenshot quotes I don't want to forget. I journal about characters who went through something I've gone through too — it sounds small, but it's genuinely one of the things that's helped me process a lot. Books have a way of narrating feelings you didn't have words for yet.

If I had to pick one favorite as an adult, it'd be Gone Girl — that kind of unreliable-narrator thriller is exactly the sort of book I can't put down. If you've got a recommendation, I'm always taking them.
