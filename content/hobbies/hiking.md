---
title: "My Journey with Hiking"
date: "2026-08-19"
description: "From getting dragged along on mountaineering club hikes to chasing sunrises at national parks."
---

Back in engineering, we had a mountaineering club on campus. I joined one event because a friend of mine was the president, and somehow that turned into getting dragged along to a few of their hikes. I did not like it. At all. It meant getting up absurdly early, a lot of planning, a lot of walking, a lot of sweating, tripping and falling more than I'd like to admit, climbing when it got steep. None of it felt fun in the moment.

Then we'd get to the top, and it would all make sense. The sunrise, the view, the air actually feeling different up there — and the kind of memory that sticks with you long after your legs stop hurting. That's the part that mattered, and it's the part that kept pulling me back even after I swore the climb itself wasn't for me.

Now I hike on my own whenever I can find the time, especially living in the US with no shortage of national parks and trails nearby. Any long weekend I get, I'll take my national park passport and go collect stamps — hike, explore, be in nature for a bit. There's a routine to it now: download offline maps before I go, since cell service disappears the second I'm on the trail; stop by the visitor center for trail conditions and permits; keep an actual paper map on hand too, since phones die. I'll read up on a trail beforehand — what other hikers said about it, what to expect, what's actually worth a detour nearby.

Recently I did a 6-mile hike at a decent pace and was genuinely impressed with how much my core strength had improved without me really tracking it. I'm even considering training for a marathon at this point.

Yosemite is my favorite so far, hands down. I'd go back in a heartbeat.
